      The method

      How Plumbline works

      A stock's price is what people pay today. Its intrinsic value is what the business is worth based on the cash and profit it is expected to make. Plumbline estimates that value in four steps.

      1. Collect the numbers

        Price, shares, cash, debt, sales, profit and cash flow for the last 12 months, plus five years of valuation ratios and analyst forecasts.

      2. Forecast the future

        Growth for the next two years comes from analyst forecasts. After that it fades step by step towards long-run economic growth (3%) over 20 years.

      3. Value it several ways

        Four cash flow and earnings models, three valuation-multiple checks and two growth-adjusted checks, each looking at the company from a different angle.

      4. Reach a verdict

        The estimates are combined using the median, so no single extreme result can skew the fair value. At 20% or more above the share price it is a BUY, 20% or more below a SELL, and anything in between a HOLD.

      Why the median

      Why not trust just one method?

      Every valuation method rests on assumptions, and each one can be wrong in its own way. Looking at several methods together shows where they agree and where they don't.

      Cash flow models

      Very sensitive to the discount rate. A small change in the rate can move the value a lot.

      Valuation multiples

      Assume the stock will trade at its usual P/E, P/S or P/B. That only holds if the past repeats.

      Growth-adjusted checks

      Compare price with growth against a benchmark, and that benchmark is partly a judgement call.

      Close togetherWhen several independent methods land in a similar place, you can trust the answer more.
      Far apartA wide spread is itself a warning that the value is hard to pin down.
      The medianTaking the middle value means one extreme method cannot drag the result up or down.

      Business quality

      How the quality score works

      A cheap stock is only a good buy if the business behind it is sound. The scorecard grades the company on 13 measures taken from its own financial statements.

      2Strong1Good0Weak Each measure earns points. The four core measures count double, because profit that turns into cash is the clearest sign of a healthy business.

      1. Earnings & cash flow

        45%

        ROIC, ROE, free cash flow to net income and free cash flow growth (the four core measures), plus net and gross profit margin.

      2. Balance sheet

        30%

        Debt to equity, debt to EBITDA, interest coverage and retained earnings. Can the company repay its debts and still fund growth?

      3. Growth outlook

        25%

        Yearly growth in earnings per share and revenue, and how consistently revenue has grown.

      Key terms

      Glossary

      Intrinsic value

      An estimate of what a business is really worth, based on the cash and profit it can produce, rather than on the day-to-day share price.

      Discount rate and WACC

      Money received in the future is worth less than money today. The discount rate converts future cash into today's value. WACC (weighted average cost of capital) blends the return shareholders expect with the cost of the company's debt. A higher WACC gives a lower value.

      Cost of equity and CAPM

      The return shareholders expect for owning a risky stock. CAPM calculates it as the risk-free rate plus beta times the equity risk premium.

      Beta

      How much a stock tends to move compared with the overall market. A beta of 2 means it has tended to move about twice as much.

      Terminal value

      The value of all the cash flows after the forecast period ends. It is estimated by assuming the company grows at a steady, modest rate forever.

      Free cash flow

      Operating cash flow minus capital spending. It is the cash left for shareholders after the company has paid to maintain and grow its business.

      Non-recurring items

      One-off gains or costs, such as a gain from selling an investment. Plumbline removes them from earnings so the valuation reflects what the business earns in a normal year.

      P/E, P/S and P/B

      Price divided by earnings, sales or book value (net assets) per share. They show how much investors pay for each dollar of each.

      PEG and PSG

      P/E or P/S divided by the growth rate. They check whether a high multiple is justified by fast growth.

      Median and trimmed mean

      The median is the middle value when all the values are sorted. The trimmed mean drops the highest and lowest values and averages the rest. Both are less affected by outliers than a plain average.

      ROIC (return on invested capital)

      After-tax operating profit divided by all the money invested in the business (shareholders' equity plus debt, minus spare cash). It shows how well management turns invested money into profit, whether that money came from owners or lenders.

      ROE (return on equity)

      Net profit divided by shareholders' equity. It shows the return on the owners' money. High debt can push ROE up artificially, so it is best read next to ROIC and debt to equity.

      EBITDA

      Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. It is a rough measure of the cash profit from operations, often used to judge how much debt a company can carry.

      Interest coverage

      Operating profit divided by interest expense. A value of 5x means profit could pay the interest bill five times over.

      Retained earnings

      All the profit a company has kept over its life instead of paying it out as dividends or buybacks. A growing balance means the company can fund new projects from its own money.
